# Product Vision Document

## What should Kivi mean in someone's life?

Kivi should mean you stop translating yourself before you speak. Most people who live between Hindi and English aren't bilingual in two neat, separate boxes. They think in a blend, and any English first tool quietly taxes them for it: pause, pick a language, restart the thought. Kivi's job is to get rid of that tax completely, and then go a step further, becoming the one interface that sounds like the actual person using it, in whatever context they're in, without them having to explain themselves twice.

## Who should it belong to?

Not "Indian users" as some demographic checkbox, but anyone whose working voice code switches naturally. The engineer who thinks in English but swears in Hindi. The founder who's formal on email and blunt on Slack. The student writing a PRD in one tab and texting a friend in the next. It belongs to people who've never used a dictation tool that didn't quietly assume they think in clean, monolingual, corporate English.

## Where does it create value competitors don't?

Every competitor we looked at is stuck fighting the same trade off. Cloud tools like Wispr Flow are fast and fluent, but they rewrite you into something smoother and more generic, and people stop trusting that once the free trial runs out. Local tools like Superwhisper are more trustworthy but demand a lot of upfront configuring and manual cleanup, meaning you're the one doing the work of teaching it who you are. Nobody has managed to be smart and yours at the same time. Kivi already has the raw pieces for this: Memory, so corrections stick, and Styles, so it can hold a different persona per app. That's not really a feature so much as a different relationship with a person's own voice. And the code switching angle isn't just a market wedge here, it's proof of the concept. A tool that can hold two languages in one register, per app, is already doing the hard version of "sound like me." Once you can do that, staying consistent in English alone is easy by comparison.

## Why would someone keep using it?

Because the cost of leaving grows every day they stay. A Kivi that's been corrected fifty times, that already knows this is "Slack casual Hinglish" and that is "Cursor technical English only," isn't something anyone wants to abandon and retrain from scratch on Wispr Flow or Superwhisper. This isn't retention built on habit. It's retention built on accumulated understanding that simply doesn't export anywhere else.

## What must it refuse to become?

Kivi has to refuse to become a general purpose AI writing assistant, the kind that offers to improve your sentence or add ideas you never asked for. The moment Kivi starts sounding smarter than the user instead of sounding more like the user, it becomes exactly the kind of tool people complain about when they say "it rewrote what I actually said," just with better branding. It also has to refuse to turn multilingual support into a settings toggle, some dropdown that says "select language: Hindi or English." That's the failure mode of every English first tool, just wearing a costume of inclusivity. Kivi's multilingual ability needs to be built into how it works, not tucked away as an option someone has to go find.
